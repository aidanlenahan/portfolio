My take on Cyberpatriot: during and reflection

October 23, 2024 Updated November 3, 2026 Aidan Lenahan

I wrote this in October 2024 as a junior. I’ve left it as it was, with an update from November 2026 at the end.

October 2024

My intrest for technology mostly sparked from one thing--Cyberpatiot. I have been doing Cyberpatriot since sixth grade. I am now a junior at Red Bank Regional and a member of the IT adacemy. Throughout the years, I have learned an abundance of information from peers, mentors, coaches, online videos, blogs like this one, and so on.

In this blog post, I will outline what I have learned, the recources I have gathered, and the recources I myself have made.

Middle School

Starting in sixth grade, I have participated in Cyberpatriot. Of course, when first starting, I was completely clueless. Thankfully, I was apart of a team of two others: let's call them Jack and Al. Jack specialized in Windows. He was very good at the Forensics Questions and getting rid of backdoors. Al specialized in Linux, which I didn't understand one bit. The prevalence of the command line was intimidating, but Al was good at it.

I was assigned the only image left: Windows Server. It wasn't too bad. If I had any questions, I could ask Jack and he would do a pretty good job with answering since Windows and Server are relatively similar.

I did average on Windows Server. I understood most of the concepts thrown at me, but only earned around 40-50 points in round 1 my first year. Although our team was relatively knowledgeable, we weren't very focused. We watched Youtube videos and Rickrolled each other during practices and even competitions. In hindsight, middle school students have a very small attention span.

Reflection

November 3, 2026

This blog post was buried in an old repo I made private more than a year ago. I've decided to reflect on CyberPatriot, as well as elaborate on this post, since there really isn't much here. Hopefully, this reflection won't have as many typos as my first iteration of this post did.

For anyone hearing about CyberPatriot for the first time, CyberPatriot is a nationwide competition for middle school and high school students to participate in securing computers and networks. It's based on a points system: the more computer vulnerabilities one can secure, the more points they earn for their team. Also, competitors are organized into teams of up to 6 people per team. Each team member specializes in securing a particular section of the competition, such as Windows, some form of Linux, Windows Server, or the Cisco Packet Tracer.

When I started in middle school, as I had said above, I was in a team with two people. Since I was the newbie, I was forced to learn how to secure Windows Server, or Windows Server 2016 at the time, to be exact. Since they are built around the same overall system, it wasn't too difficult, since it was mostly similar to the consumer version of Microsoft Windows. But there were still differences, like how pretty much everything was to be configured through Server Manager, and if it was Active Directory-enabled, also through ADUC. This forced me to step outside the box. It was new, and parts of it were discouraging, especially towards the later rounds in the season, where it was meant to be tougher. But I still had fun, and with every point, I got to hear the euphoric gain.wav, which was the sound the scoring engine would play when you gained points.

The competitions were also very fun, as there was a little nerdy community of passionate kids ready to get more points. In middle school, the CyberPatriot club had between 4 to 6 teams per year. As I said before, everyone was passionate, but everyone was also never super focused. During this time, a competition lasted 6 hours, which is a lot to ask for the attention span of anyone, let alone middle schoolers.

In high school, CyberPatriot got a little more serious. Instead of the main goal being to have fun, that also now coexisted with wanting to make it to the Nationals round. The advising from the club advisor was more professional, and competitors were taking it more seriously. Around this time was when I started to take the existing knowledge I had, and use it to help automate tasks that would take a lot of time during the competition, which would help free my time to do other important things that couldn't be as automated. This became increasingly important during my sophomore year of high school, where they decreased the competition time to 4 hours, down from 6.

Something I learned while developing these scripts to automate certain parts of the competition was that you really can never make something too good or optimized. Every time I used my scripts, I always found a problem or something I could improve. Whether it was ease of use, making something more intuitive, or actually fixing a critical bug in the script, each time I used the scripts after previously fixing old problems, more would appear. It could never be perfect, even after 2 years of developing and improving constantly.

What made it difficult were two things: I was trying to shoot at a moving target, and the general premise that something can always be improved. What I mean by trying to shoot a moving target is this: the CyberPatriot team responsible for developing the competitions is also constantly improving the competition, whether that means making something harder to fix, making tasks less scriptable, or making it harder to harness AI to a competitor's advantage. This dynamic between competitors and CyberPatriot themselves also made it seem like you were competing against the maintenance team in addition to the competition itself. Each year I returned to CyberPatriot, I knew that I couldn't overly rely on my experiences from past competitions, as the maintenance team learned from what they did in the past, and either did something different or made the existing aspect harder.

All of the guides and scripts I made are now public. You can find the guides at cyberpatriot.alenahan.net, and the source code, along with my older scripts, in my CyberPatriot GitHub repository.

As I got further into high school with the same CyberPatriot team (more or less), I became more of an expert. I put in lots of hours trying to optimize scripts, do research on my part of the competition, and show up to practices. Through all of this, I became the unofficial team captain of my team. If anyone was struggling with their part of the competition (i.e. Linux, Windows, Cisco Packet Tracer), they'd ask me, and I would switch to their part and help them figure it out. This dynamic ended up working out relatively well, except for one thing. My teammates became too dependent on me, to the point where they were taking me away from my part more than they were doing theirs, which made me more of a one-man band than a good leader. In hindsight, a good leader would have urged their teammate to figure it out on their own, practice on their own time, and hone in on the skills they need to know. As a result, this dynamic would have allowed me to focus my efforts on my part of the competition, my teammates on theirs, and cultivate an environment amongst my team where they want to put in the effort so they can hold their own. Now, that doesn't mean a team leader should always turn down requests for help, especially in a timed competition with high stakes. But, there is most certainly a sweet spot to how often that should happen.

I always thought of myself as the most passionate for CyberPatriot in my team. I realized that a good dynamic will naturally set itself in place if every team member's passion levels are more or less the same, and somewhat high. It was hard to get teammates to want to put in the work, because you either want to, or you don't. Concepts can be taught. Approaches can be learned. But effort and passion can't be taught. It's funny, this is more or less the same speech I've heard quite a few times during my time as a lacrosse player, across a handful of teams. Because it's true, and it's universal, whether it's a nationwide cyber competition or a sport of some kind.

The idea that teammates are more compatible with one another if they have about the same passion level for something isn't strictly confined to teams. As I am now a freshman in college, I was picking a host of clubs to try out not more than three months ago. I discovered that a similar thing applies, which is what helped me pick the clubs I knew I'd prefer.

Getting further into high school with CyberPatriot also inspired me to volunteer as a coach for the feeder CyberPatriot middle school program I came from. I really liked working with passionate kids, as I saw myself in them. Not to be corny, but there was something different about being someone's "why" to want to keep doing something that they're passionate about. I had people like that for me along my journey with CyberPatriot, and it was truly awesome to be that person for other passionate kids. I got to see how unserious kids were in comparison to high school competitors, but it was nice, as I was forced to adjust my existing guides, materials, and ways of explaining things so it would resonate with them.

Now being in college, I've grown to miss CyberPatriot a lot. It was a community, but also so much more. It was something I'd work at whether in school, on summer break, during the week, or on weekends. It was always waiting for me from school year to school year, and the passion I grew for it led me to make a series of decisions which led me to where I am now, and I couldn't be more grateful. My middle school CyberPatriot advisor urged me to apply to the Information Technology Academy at my local high school. Once in that, I took a bunch of computer science and IT classes, which sparked more interest, leading me to start many personal projects and get involved in more relevant activities, such as internships and volunteering. With these experiences, I applied to 10 universities as a computer science major, and got into all of them.

CyberPatriot has taught me to take incoming opportunities with open arms and explore as much as I can, as that future opportunity might turn into the next CyberPatriot for me down the line.

Thanks for reading.
